Frequently asked questions

Answers to common questions about native wildlife and this site.

Native means the species got here on its own, without people bringing it. Some, like the tūī, live only in New Zealand. Others arrived naturally but also live elsewhere in the world.

It's not about the animals themselves, they didn't choose to be here. It's that a lot of introduced species compete with natives for food and nesting space, or prey on them directly. Cats and rats are the biggest examples.

Yes. Birds like tūī, fantail and kererū move through suburban gardens all the time, not just forest. Small things like planting a native shrub or keeping your cat inside at night still make a real difference wherever you live.

Feeding bread or seed mostly attracts introduced birds like sparrows and starlings, not natives. If you want to support native birds specifically, planting nectar or fruit producing native plants works a lot better than a feeder.

Cats are one of the biggest threats to native birds and lizards, especially young birds that haven't learned to fly properly yet. Keeping your cat inside at night, when most hunting happens, is one of the easiest changes you can make.

Species information is based on publicly available conservation resources, including the Department of Conservation and Auckland Council. Sources are credited in the site documentation.

New Zealand uses a conservation status system that looks at things like population size and how fast it's dropping. On this site it's simplified into three groups, native, threatened, and introduced, just to keep things easy to scan.

This site was built as a school project, so it's not taking submissions right now. But if you're curious about a species that isn't listed, the DOC website is a good place to check.
